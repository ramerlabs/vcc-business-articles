# How Agencies Can buy VCC with crypto Without Losing Approval Control

<p>If your team needs to buy VCC with crypto, treat the purchase as a controlled funding workflow—not as an individual employee expense. The safest operating model is to separate requester, approver, funder, and card user roles; document the business purpose; set spending limits before money is deposited; and reconcile every transaction to a campaign, tool, client, or supplier.</p><p>A good team SOP should answer five questions before anyone moves funds: who may request a card, what evidence is required, who approves the deposit, how much may be funded, and what happens when a card is declined, compromised, or no longer needed. This structure works for agencies, e-commerce teams, freelancers with contractors, and SaaS operators using cards for advertising, subscriptions, and online suppliers.</p><p>Crypto funding adds an additional control point. The team should verify the provider’s supported asset and network, confirm wallet ownership or authorization, record the transaction hash, and avoid assuming that crypto settlement makes a payment anonymous or exempt from identity checks. The objective is operational clarity, not evasion of platform, financial, tax, or compliance requirements.</p><h2>Build the SOP around roles, limits, and evidence</h2><p>Start by assigning four roles, even if one person temporarily holds more than one role in a small business. The <strong>requester</strong> explains why the card is needed. The <strong>approver</strong> confirms that the request fits budget and policy. The <strong>funder</strong> sends crypto or another permitted funding method and records the deposit. The <strong>card administrator</strong> creates, assigns, monitors, freezes, or closes the card.</p><p>Separating these duties reduces preventable errors. A media buyer should not be able to request a large deposit, approve it, fund it, and use the card without review. In a two-person company, the founder may approve and fund while the operator uses the card, but that exception should be written down rather than left implicit.</p><p>Every request should contain the same minimum fields:</p><ul><li>Requester name, team, and card user.</li><li>Business purpose, such as a specific ad account, software subscription, marketplace, or supplier.</li><li>Merchant category and expected billing frequency.</li><li>Requested spending limit and deposit amount.</li><li>Start date, review date, and planned end date.</li><li>Client, project, cost center, or campaign reference.</li><li>Reason a new card is needed instead of an existing card.</li></ul><p>Do not approve a request that only says “marketing” or “tools.” That description is too broad for later reconciliation. “Card for Client A search campaign, capped at approved weekly budget, reviewed every Friday” is specific enough to support a decision.</p><h2>Use a written request-to-deposit workflow</h2><p>The core workflow should be predictable enough that a new contractor can follow it without a private explanation. A practical sequence is request, review, approval, deposit, activation, use, reconciliation, and closure.</p><ol><li>The requester submits the card request with purpose, amount, duration, and supporting evidence such as an ad budget, invoice, checkout page, or subscription quote.</li><li>The approver checks the request against the current budget, client authorization, platform rules, and the team’s maximum card limit.</li><li>The card administrator confirms whether an existing card can serve the purpose. If not, the administrator creates or selects a dedicated card.</li><li>The funder verifies the destination details through the provider’s authenticated dashboard or approved process. Do not rely on an address copied from an unverified chat message.</li><li>The funder sends the approved amount, records the asset, network, amount, date, wallet label, and transaction hash, and waits for the provider’s stated confirmation process.</li><li>The administrator confirms that the balance and card status are correct before sharing card details with the user.</li><li>The card user spends only for the approved purpose and uploads receipts or transaction notes on the agreed schedule.</li><li>The administrator reconciles activity, flags exceptions, and freezes or closes the card when the project ends.</li></ol><p>If your team is researching how the initial funding works, keep the provider’s product documentation beside the internal SOP. A small, short-lived card for a known software subscription is different from a large advertising deposit that could be spent rapidly. Define thresholds in your currency of record and review the equivalent crypto amount at the time of funding.</p><p>One workable policy is to use three levels. <strong>Level one</strong> covers routine requests within an already approved budget and can be approved by a team lead. <strong>Level two</strong> covers new merchants, higher limits, recurring billing, or a new client and requires finance or founder approval. <strong>Level three</strong> covers unusually large deposits, unfamiliar platforms, changes to wallet procedures, or urgent exceptions and requires two approvers.</p><p>Do not use a single threshold for every activity. Advertising deserves a lower exposure window because spend can accelerate quickly. A recurring SaaS card may need a higher total balance but a lower monthly limit. A supplier card may need temporary capacity for a purchase order but should be frozen after the order is complete.</p><p>Require a second review when any of these conditions apply:</p><ul><li>The card will be used for a new merchant or a platform with unclear billing behavior.</li><li>The requested deposit exceeds the approved campaign or project budget.</li><li>The card will be shared by multiple users.</li><li>The card is intended for recurring payments or preauthorization holds.</li><li>The request is urgent and skips ordinary documentation.</li><li>The funding wallet, network, or asset differs from the team’s standard procedure.</li></ul><h2>Choose card types by use case, not convenience</h2><p>A disposable or single-purpose virtual card can be appropriate for a one-time supplier charge, a trial that the team plans to cancel, or a short experiment where continued billing is not wanted. A user may request a card for one month, but the merchant may retain the number and attempt future charges. Your SOP should therefore record the merchant’s renewal date, expected amount, cancellation owner, and evidence of cancellation.</p><p>For a recurring subscription, the request should state whether the card is intended to remain active indefinitely, through a fixed project end date, or only until a usage threshold is reached. A clear purpose lowers uncertainty. High spend velocity increases the required approval level. A reversible subscription is less risky than an irreversible supplier payment. Continuity matters when a decline would stop revenue-generating activity.</p><p>Use this A-versus-B rule in team discussions. Choose <strong>one dedicated card with a controlled reload</strong> when the merchant is known, the budget is documented, the activity is recurring, and continuity matters. Separate cards improve attribution and limit exposure, but too many cards create administrative overhead and can make reconciliation harder. Issue separate cards when users have different clients, merchants, budgets, or risk profiles. A shared card may be acceptable for a tightly controlled team only when access, purpose, limits, and receipt submission are clearly documented.</p><h3>How should the SOP handle a failed crypto deposit?</h3><p>Pause activation and do not send a second payment immediately. Check the asset, network, destination, amount, confirmation status, and provider instructions. Record the transaction hash and contact the provider through its official support channel. Escalate internally if the amount is material or the address may be wrong. Keep the incident attached to the original request so later reviewers can see what happened.</p><h3>When should a reloadable card be replaced with a new card?</h3><p>Replace or separate the card when the purpose changes materially, several unrelated merchants are added, credentials may have been exposed, billing behavior becomes unpredictable, or reconciliation repeatedly fails.
