# How to Build a Per-Client Policy for Business Virtual Cards

<p>The safest way to manage client spending is to give each client, campaign, or approved project its own controlled card profile instead of sharing one company card across an entire team. A good per-client policy defines who may request a card, what the card can pay for, how much it can spend, who approves changes, and what happens when the engagement ends.</p>
<p>Use <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/">business virtual cards</a> as a payment-control layer, not as a substitute for accounting or client approval. The policy should connect every transaction to a client, budget, campaign, and owner. That makes reconciliation easier, limits accidental overspending, and creates a clear record when a subscription renews or an advertising platform rejects a payment.</p>
<h2>Start with one card policy per client or controlled spending purpose</h2>
<p>A per-client card policy is a written set of rules for issuing and operating a virtual card. It should answer practical questions before money leaves the account: Is the card for advertising, software, suppliers, travel, or a one-time purchase? What is the approved budget? Is the limit daily, monthly, or tied to a project milestone? Who can use the card, and who reviews the transactions?</p>
<p>The best structure is usually one of the following:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>One card per client:</strong> useful when an agency manages several recurring services for the same client and wants simple client-level reporting.</li>
<li><strong>One card per campaign:</strong> useful for media buying, where each card maps to a platform account, campaign group, or launch budget.</li>
<li><strong>One card per vendor category:</strong> useful for SaaS, logistics, or procurement when multiple clients share the same operational process.</li>
<li><strong>One card per project or purchase order:</strong> useful when spending must stop automatically after a defined deliverable or supplier order.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not create a separate card for every employee or small purchase unless the added administration is justified. Too many cards can make ownership unclear, increase reconciliation work, and encourage people to treat card creation as a casual workaround for approval requirements.</p>
<h2>Choose the right control model: client card, campaign card, or shared category card</h2>
<p>There is no universal best model. Choose based on the type of risk you are trying to control. If the main risk is client confusion, use a client-specific card. If the main risk is campaign overspend, use a campaign-specific card. If the main risk is unauthorized software renewals, use a category card with a tight merchant and limit policy.</p>
<blockquote><strong>Decision framework:</strong> Choose a client card when reporting and reimbursement are the priority; choose a campaign card when budget pacing is the priority; choose a category card when vendor control and recurring billing are the priority. If two risks are equally important, use separate cards rather than forcing one card to satisfy incompatible controls.</blockquote>
<p>For ongoing balances or repeated funding, review whether a <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-vcc">reloadable vcc</a> fits the operating model. A reloadable product may be useful when a team needs to add funds within an approved budget instead of repeatedly requesting a new card. It still needs funding authorization, transaction monitoring, and a documented stop procedure.</p>
<p>For recurring software, hosting, or advertising charges, check the provider rules carefully before changing card details. A guide to <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-card-recurring-payments">virtual card recurring payments</a> can help your team think through renewal behavior, failed charges, card replacement, and the difference between a temporary card and a payment method intended to remain active.</p>
<h2>Define the policy fields that prevent ambiguity</h2>
<p>A useful template should be specific enough that another administrator can enforce it without asking the account owner for interpretation. Store the policy with the client record, card record, and accounting reference. At minimum, include these fields:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Client and project:</strong> legal or trading name, internal client ID, project name, and contract or purchase-order reference.</li>
<li><strong>Business purpose:</strong> the exact approved use, such as paid search, email software, fulfillment, or a supplier deposit.</li>
<li><strong>Card owner:</strong> the employee or contractor responsible for daily use and receipt collection.</li>
<li><strong>Approver:</strong> the person who can approve issuance, limit increases, merchant exceptions, and continued use.</li>
<li><strong>Funding source:</strong> the company account or client-funded balance used to support the card.</li>
<li><strong>Limit and period:</strong> daily, weekly, monthly, total project, or another clearly defined cap.</li>
<li><strong>Merchant scope:</strong> allowed vendors, platforms, countries, currencies, and transaction types.</li>
<li><strong>Review date:</strong> the date for checking spend, access, limits, and whether the card is still required.</li>
<li><strong>End condition:</strong> a contract end date, campaign end, budget exhaustion, vendor cancellation, or manual close approval.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use plain language for exceptions. For example, write that a card may pay for the named advertising platform and its approved tax or verification charge, but may not pay for personal purchases, unrelated client work, cash-equivalent transactions, or a new vendor without written approval.</p>
<h2>Set limits that match the spending pattern, not just the total budget</h2>
<p>A total budget alone is not enough. A card with a large monthly limit can still create a serious cash-flow problem if a vendor bills the full amount immediately. Use multiple controls where available: per-transaction limits, daily limits, monthly limits, merchant restrictions, geographic restrictions, and expiration or review dates.</p>
<p>For media buying, separate the approved campaign budget from the card limit. The limit should account for expected platform billing behavior, taxes, refunds, and a small operational buffer approved by finance. It should not be so high that a billing error can consume the entire client budget before anyone notices. Require a documented limit increase when spend changes materially.</p>
<p>For SaaS, use a lower recurring limit and identify the billing owner. A subscription card should not be treated as permission to add seats, upgrade plans, or buy unrelated services. Those changes should follow the same purchase approval process as a new vendor.</p>
<p>For suppliers, consider whether a one-time or short-expiry card is safer than a reusable card. A reusable card is more convenient for repeat orders, but a one-time card reduces the risk of a vendor retaining payment details after the order is complete. When a supplier requires repeated funding, a <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-credit-card">reloadable virtual credit card</a> may be considered, provided the reload authority and maximum balance are written into the policy.</p>
<h2>Use a repeatable request, approval, and closure workflow</h2>
<p>Keep card administration out of informal chat wherever possible. A simple ticket, form, or shared approval system should capture the request and preserve the decision. The workflow can be short, but it must have clear ownership.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Request:</strong> the requester submits the client, purpose, vendor, budget, start date, end date, and expected billing pattern.</li>
<li><strong>Validate:</strong> the account owner confirms that the spend is covered by the client agreement, purchase order, or internal budget.</li>
<li><strong>Approve:</strong> an authorized approver confirms the limit, merchant scope, and card type. The requester should not be the only approver for their own spend.</li>
<li><strong>Issue:</strong> an administrator creates or assigns the card, records its identifier, and applies the approved controls.</li>
<li><strong>Use:</strong> the cardholder attaches receipts, invoices, campaign references, or order records as soon as practical.</li>
<li><strong>Review:</strong> finance or the client owner checks transactions against the policy at an agreed cadence.</li>
<li><strong>Close:</strong> the administrator freezes or closes the card when the end condition occurs and records the reason.</li>
</ol>
<p>Separate duties when the spend is significant. The person running ads may request a budget, but a finance lead or account director should approve the limit. For a small team, full separation may be impractical; in that case, use a second-person review and a periodic export of transactions.</p>
<h2>Copy this per-client card policy template</h2>
<p>Adapt the following text to your team, provider, contract terms, and local accounting process. It is an operational template, not legal or financial advice.</p>
<blockquote><strong>Per-client card policy</strong><br>Client and project: [name and internal ID].<br>Card purpose: [specific approved use].<br>Card type: [one-time, reusable, reloadable, or recurring].<br>Authorized user: [name, role, and contact].<br>Approver: [name and role].<br>Approved merchants and platforms: [list].<br>Approved budget: [amount, currency, and budget period].<br>Card controls: [transaction limit, daily limit, monthly limit, merchant or geographic restrictions, and expiry or review date].<br>Required evidence: [receipt, invoice, campaign ID, order number, or client approval].<br>Prohibited use: personal expenses, cash-equivalent transactions, unapproved vendors, unrelated client work, and any purchase outside the stated purpose.<br>Limit changes: require written approval from [role] and must state the reason, new amount, and effective date.<br>Incident procedure: report unauthorized, duplicated, disputed, or unexpected transactions to [team] within [internal timeframe]. Freeze the card when appropriate and preserve transaction evidence.<br>Closure condition: [date, campaign end, contract event, or budget exhaustion]. The administrator must freeze or close the card, remove access, and record the closure date.<br>Review schedule: [weekly, monthly, or at milestone]. Policy owner: [name and role].</blockquote>
<p>Keep the template versioned. When a limit changes, do not overwrite the old approval without a record. Note who approved the change, why it was needed, and when it should expire. This is especially important when a client disputes a charge or asks why spend exceeded an earlier forecast.</p>
<h2>Make recurring payments and reloads part of the control design</h2>
<p>Recurring billing is where otherwise sensible card policies often fail. A subscription may renew after a client leaves, a platform may retry a failed charge, or a vendor may authorize a small verification transaction before billing the main amount. Your policy should state whether these events are permitted and who monitors them.</p>
<p>For subscriptions, maintain a separate register containing the vendor, plan name, billing frequency, renewal date, card identifier, owner, cancellation instructions, and client allocation. Reconcile the register against card transactions at least monthly. If the vendor stores the card, document who may update the payment method and whether a replacement card will continue the subscription.</p>
<p>For reloadable products, define who can add funds, the maximum reload amount, the evidence required, and whether unused funds must be returned or reassigned. A <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-card">reloadable virtual card</a> can simplify repeat spending, but it should not become an open-ended pool that multiple clients or departments use without allocation records.</p>
<p>If the business specifically requires a Visa-branded product, review the product terms and acceptance conditions rather than assuming every online merchant will process it identically. Resources on a <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-visa-reloadable">virtual visa reloadable</a> option can be part of that research, but eligibility, verification, funding, merchant acceptance, and fees should be confirmed before rollout.</p>
<h2>Run this implementation checklist before issuing cards</h2>
<p>Use this checklist for every new client card and repeat it when a card changes purpose:</p>
<ul>
<li>Confirm the client, project, contract, and internal accounting code.</li>
<li>Write a one-sentence purpose that names the permitted spend.</li>
<li>Choose one-time, recurring, reusable, or reloadable operation based on the billing pattern.</li>
<li>Set transaction, daily, monthly, and total project limits where supported.</li>
<li>Record authorized merchants, currencies, countries, and prohibited categories.</li>
<li>Assign a cardholder, approver, policy owner, and backup administrator.</li>
<li>Set receipt requirements, review frequency, renewal monitoring, and the closure trigger.</li>
<li>Test the card with an approved transaction and confirm that the accounting reference is captured.</li>
</ul>
<p>After issuance, schedule the first review sooner than the normal cadence. An early check can reveal that the vendor bills at a different time, the platform requires a larger verification amount, or the cardholder cannot attach the required evidence. Fix those issues before the card becomes embedded in a critical workflow.</p>
<h2>Avoid these common policy mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sharing one card across unrelated clients:</strong> this makes attribution difficult and increases the impact of an unauthorized charge.</li>
<li><strong>Using only a monthly limit:</strong> a large monthly ceiling may not prevent a damaging single transaction or rapid series of charges.</li>
<li><strong>Failing to define the owner:</strong> when everyone is responsible, no one is reliably checking receipts, renewals, or exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>Allowing verbal limit increases:</strong> informal approvals are easily forgotten and hard to reconcile during a dispute.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring the end date:</strong> cards often remain active after campaigns, contracts, or subscriptions have ended.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming reloadable means unlimited:</strong> reload authority must be restricted just like initial issuance.</li>
<li><strong>Replacing a card without checking recurring vendors:</strong> a replacement may interrupt legitimate services or leave an old payment method active in a vendor account.</li>
<li><strong>Collecting receipts but not client references:</strong> a receipt proves a purchase occurred, but not which client or approved purpose it served.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not use a per-client card policy to bypass a platform’s identity, advertising, merchant, or payment rules. It is also a poor fit when a client requires direct ownership of the payment account, when the provider prohibits third-party funding, or when the transaction is too complex for reliable automated reconciliation. In those cases, use the client’s approved payment method or obtain written operational guidance before proceeding.</p>
<h2>FAQ: practical questions about per-client card policies</h2>
<h3>Should every client receive a separate virtual card?</h3>
<p>Not necessarily. Separate cards are most useful when clients have different budgets, billing responsibilities, vendors, or reporting requirements. A shared category card can work for low-risk internal software if every transaction has a required client code and the vendor relationship is centrally managed. Do not share a card merely for convenience when it would make disputes, refunds, or client invoicing difficult to resolve.</p>
<h3>What limit should a client card have?</h3>
<p>Set the limit from the expected billing pattern and approved budget, not from a generic company-wide number. Consider the largest legitimate transaction, billing retries, taxes, refunds, and a controlled buffer. For campaign spend, use a limit that allows normal pacing but would trigger review before the entire budget is exposed. Reassess the limit when spend, vendor terms, or the client authorization changes.</p>
<h3>Are reloadable virtual cards better for agencies?</h3>
<p>They can be useful for repeat spending when the agency needs to add funds within a defined budget and wants to avoid issuing a new card for every cycle. They are not automatically better: reload permissions, balance ownership, refunds, merchant acceptance, and reconciliation require additional controls. Use them when the operational benefit is clear, and document who may reload, how much, and for which client or project.</p>
<h3>How often should transactions be reviewed?</h3>
<p>Review frequency should follow risk and transaction volume. High-volume advertising or supplier cards may need daily monitoring, while a low-volume software card may be reviewed monthly. In every case, review after a new card is issued, after a limit increase, and before a renewal or contract end. Match transactions to receipts, client references, expected vendors, and the approved budget.</p>
<h3>What should happen when a client relationship ends?</h3>
<p>Stop new spend, freeze or close the card, cancel or reassign authorized subscriptions, remove user access, and record the closure date. Reconcile pending authorizations, refunds, chargebacks, and unused balances before final client billing. Keep the policy and transaction records according to your accounting and contractual retention requirements, while limiting access to people who need them.</p>
<h2>Take these next steps in the next seven days</h2>
<p>On day one, list every active card and assign each to a client, project, or internal category. On day two, identify cards with shared users, unclear limits, or no end date. On day three, choose your standard template and have finance or an account lead approve the required fields.</p>
<p>On days four and five, migrate one low-risk client workflow first. Apply the card controls, test an approved transaction, and confirm that receipts and client references reach the accounting system. On day six, review recurring vendors, reload permissions, and closure triggers. On day seven, document what failed, revise the template, and set a recurring review calendar.</p>
<p>The goal is not to create paperwork for its own sake. It is to make every card explainable: who authorized it, what it can pay for, how much it can spend, and when it stops. That standard gives freelancers, agencies, e-commerce teams, and media buyers a practical way to use virtual payment tools without losing control of client money.</p>
