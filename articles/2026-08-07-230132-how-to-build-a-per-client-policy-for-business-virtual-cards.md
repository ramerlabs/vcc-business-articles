# How to Build a Per-Client Policy for Business Virtual Cards

<p>A per-client card policy gives each client, project, or advertising account a defined spending boundary instead of forcing your team to share one payment method. The practical recommendation is to issue one business virtual card per meaningful cost center, assign an owner, set a limit and expiry rule, and require a short approval record before the card is created or funded.</p><p>Use <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/">business virtual cards</a> when you need cleaner bookkeeping, faster access for contractors, or tighter control over subscriptions, advertising spend, and supplier payments. Do not treat a virtual card as a replacement for client contracts, accounting controls, or platform compliance. It is a payment-control layer that works best when the written policy explains who may spend, what they may buy, how much they may spend, and what happens when the engagement changes.</p><h2>Define the card’s purpose before issuing it</h2><p>The first line of a per-client card policy should identify why the card exists. “Client expenses” is too broad. A useful purpose statement names the client, business activity, payment category, and expected duration.</p><p>For example: “This card is for approved advertising purchases for Client A’s search campaign from 1 April through 30 June. It may not be used for payroll, personal expenses, unrelated software, cash-equivalent purchases, or another client’s account.” This wording helps a contractor understand the boundary without interpreting a general finance rule.</p><p>Most agencies and online operators can group cards into four practical categories:</p><ul><li><strong>Advertising cards:</strong> Used for ad platforms, campaign tools, and approved promotional services.</li><li><strong>Software cards:</strong> Used for a named SaaS product or a small group of tools supporting one client.</li><li><strong>Supplier cards:</strong> Used for inventory, fulfillment, contractors, or other approved vendors.</li><li><strong>Project or launch cards:</strong> Used for a temporary initiative with a defined start date, end date, and spending ceiling.</li></ul><p>Separate categories matter because the risk is different. A recurring software card may need a low permanent limit and a subscription review. An advertising card may need frequent top-ups, daily monitoring, and a stronger approval process because spend can accelerate quickly.</p><h2>Use one policy structure for every client</h2><p>A template is valuable only if every team member fills it out consistently. Keep the policy short enough to use during a busy onboarding, but specific enough to answer an audit question later. The following fields cover the operational minimum:</p><ul><li><strong>Client and legal entity:</strong> Record the client name, billing entity, and internal client code.</li><li><strong>Business purpose:</strong> State the approved use in one or two sentences.</li><li><strong>Card owner:</strong> Name the employee, contractor, or team lead responsible for transactions.</li><li><strong>Approved merchants:</strong> List exact vendors, platforms, or merchant categories where possible.</li><li><strong>Budget:</strong> Set a total limit, a period limit, or both.</li><li><strong>Funding method:</strong> Explain who approves funding and whether the card is topped up from company funds or an approved client balance.</li><li><strong>Validity period:</strong> Include the issue date, review date, and automatic closure or suspension trigger.</li><li><strong>Evidence requirements:</strong> Specify receipts, invoices, campaign IDs, purchase orders, or screenshots required after each transaction.</li></ul><p>Write the policy so the card owner can answer three questions immediately: “Can I use this card for this purchase?”, “What is my remaining authority?”, and “What evidence must I submit?” If the answer depends on an unwritten conversation, the policy is incomplete.</p><h2>Choose card limits using a simple decision framework</h2><p>Do not select limits by copying the client’s monthly retainer or by giving every employee the same amount. Set the limit from the spending pattern and the cost of an error. A card used for a fixed monthly tool can have a different control design from a card used for live advertising.</p><p><strong>Use a low fixed limit when</strong> the merchant charge is predictable, recurring, and easy to verify. A software subscription, domain renewal, or modest supplier account generally fits this model. The limit should cover the expected charge plus a reasonable buffer, not several months of uncontrolled spending.</p><p><strong>Use a reloadable or periodically funded card when</strong> the expense changes by campaign, launch, or purchase order. A <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-vcc">reloadable vcc</a> can support staged funding, but the reload process should still require an approved amount, a named purpose, and a record of who authorized it.</p><p><strong>Use a temporary card when</strong> the engagement has a short duration, the vendor is unfamiliar, or the card will be used for a single transaction. Temporary access reduces the chance that an old card remains attached to an account after a contractor or client relationship changes.</p><p><strong>Use a higher limit only when</strong> the business can monitor the account frequently and recover from a mistaken charge. A higher limit is not automatically more efficient. It increases the amount that can be spent before someone notices a problem.</p><p>A useful comparison is fixed-limit versus reloadable control. A fixed-limit card is simpler for predictable recurring charges and requires less administration. A reloadable card is more flexible for variable campaign or supplier spend, but it creates an additional approval step for each funding event. Choose fixed limits for stability; choose reloadable funding for changing spend; choose temporary access when the main risk is duration.</p><h2>Control recurring payments and card changes</h2><p>Recurring charges are where many card policies fail. A card may start as a small software expense and later accumulate add-ons, usage fees, annual upgrades, or a second product under the same merchant account. The policy should therefore identify the approved subscription, billing frequency, expected amount, cancellation owner, and review date.</p><p>Before attaching a card to a subscription, record the account email, workspace or project name, billing cycle, renewal date, and cancellation procedure. If a vendor offers multiple products, state the exact product rather than approving the vendor generally. “Approved for Vendor X” can unintentionally authorize every upgrade and add-on available through that account.</p><p>For a more detailed operating model, review guidance on <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-card-recurring-payments">virtual card recurring payments</a>. Your internal policy should also say whether the card may be used for annual prepayments, automatic upgrades, free-trial conversions, and usage-based charges.</p><p>When a client pauses work, suspend the card before the final invoice is issued. When a client leaves, remove the card from third-party dashboards and confirm that subscriptions have been cancelled or transferred. Card closure alone may not stop a merchant from attempting another payment through an account-level billing arrangement.</p><h2>Set approval, documentation, and reconciliation rules</h2><p>A card policy should make approval proportional to risk. Requiring director approval for every small software charge creates workarounds; allowing unrestricted ad funding creates exposure. Establish thresholds and exceptions in advance.</p><ul><li>Routine charges within the approved policy may be made by the named owner.</li><li>New merchants, new subscriptions, or upgrades require written approval before payment.</li><li>Any increase above the card’s limit requires a recorded funding request.</li><li>Refunds, credits, disputed charges, and failed payments must be reported to the finance owner.</li><li>Personal purchases are prohibited, even if the employee intends to reimburse the business.</li><li>Receipts and business purpose notes should be submitted within the company’s normal expense timetable.</li></ul><p>For advertising, require the campaign name, platform account, date range, and approved budget in the funding request. For ecommerce suppliers, require the purchase order or inventory record. For software, require the user or team that benefits from the tool and the expected cancellation date.</p><p>Reconcile cards at least monthly, and more often for high-volume media buying. Compare the provider statement with internal records, platform dashboards, receipts, refunds, and client invoices. The goal is not merely to confirm that the amount is correct; it is to confirm that the charge belongs to the right client and cost center.</p><h2>Use reloadable cards without creating a funding loophole</h2><p>Reloadable products can be useful when a client’s approved budget changes during a campaign. A <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-credit-card">reloadable virtual credit card</a> may offer more operational flexibility than repeatedly creating new cards, but the policy must prevent “reload first, explain later” behavior.</p><p>Every reload request should include the requested amount, client, campaign or purchase purpose, period covered, and approving person. If the amount is not spent, the owner should report the balance and explain whether it will remain available, be returned to the funding pool, or be assigned to an approved future use.</p><p>For teams that need a reusable payment instrument for controlled online purchases, a <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-card">reloadable virtual card</a> may fit the workflow. Review provider terms carefully, including identity verification, supported merchants, funding sources, transaction limits, refund handling, and geographic availability. Product names can sound similar while offering different controls.</p><p>Do not use reloadable funding to bypass a client’s purchase approval, a platform’s payment rules, or your own accounting process. It is a budgeting mechanism, not a way to conceal the payer or avoid required verification.</p><h2>Apply the policy across contractors, agencies, and clients</h2><p>Different users need different access. A full-time finance manager may be allowed to create cards, approve reloads, and close accounts. A contractor may only view card details or request funding. A media buyer may use an approved advertising card but should not be able to change the legal billing profile of the ad platform.</p><p>Use role-based permissions wherever the provider supports them. Separate the person requesting a card from the person approving it for higher-risk expenses. If the team is very small and separation is impossible, add a compensating control: a weekly review by the owner, a low default limit, or automatic suspension after a defined period.</p><p>For client-funded work, put the payment arrangement in the client agreement or written statement of work. Clarify whether the client pays the vendor directly, reimburses the agency, or provides a budget that the agency manages. Also clarify who absorbs chargebacks, foreign exchange differences, taxes, refunds, and unauthorized transactions.</p><p>A <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-visa-reloadable">virtual visa reloadable</a> product may be considered where Visa acceptance and reload functionality match the business use case, but acceptance is not universal. Confirm the provider’s terms and test a low-value transaction before attaching the card to a critical recurring account.</p><h2>Per-client card policy template and implementation checklist</h2><p>Copy the following structure into your finance handbook or client onboarding system. Adapt the language to your contracts, provider capabilities, and local accounting requirements.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Card policy:</strong> Card [identifier] is assigned to [client/legal entity] for [specific purpose]. The authorized user is [name]. Approved merchants are [list]. The spending limit is [amount or rule] for [period]. Any reload or limit increase requires approval from [role]. Receipts and business purpose notes are due within [timeframe]. The card expires, is suspended, or is reviewed on [date or trigger]. Personal purchases, cash-equivalent transactions, unapproved vendors, and unrelated client expenses are prohibited. At engagement end or policy breach, the card owner must stop use, remove the card from relevant accounts, and notify [finance owner].</p></blockquote><p>Before activating a card, complete this checklist:</p><ol><li>Confirm the client, legal entity, project, and cost center.</li><li>Write a narrow business purpose and list approved merchants.</li><li>Assign one accountable card owner and one policy approver.</li><li>Set the initial limit, reload rule, expiry date, and review date.</li><li>Record the documentation required for each transaction.</li><li>Test one low-value payment where merchant acceptance is uncertain.</li><li>Schedule a recurring review and define the closure process.</li><li>Store the policy and approval evidence where finance can retrieve it.</li></ol><p>Keep the completed policy with the client record rather than in a private chat thread. A centralized location makes handoffs, audits, refunds, and employee departures easier to manage.</p><h2>Avoid the mistakes that make card controls ineffective</h2><p>Even a well-designed card program can fail through small operational shortcuts. Watch for these common mistakes:</p><ul><li><strong>One card for every client:</strong> This makes allocation, disputes, and responsibility difficult to establish.</li><li><strong>Vague merchant approval:</strong> Approving a vendor without naming the product or account can permit upgrades and unrelated purchases.</li><li><strong>No expiry or review date:</strong> Dormant cards often remain attached to services after a project ends.</li><li><strong>Limits based on convenience:</strong> A generous limit may hide overspending instead of preventing it.</li><li><strong>Missing receipts:</strong> A transaction amount alone does not explain its business purpose or client allocation.</li><li><strong>Uncontrolled reloads:</strong> Repeated funding without a request and approval trail turns a budget tool into an open spending channel.</li><li><strong>Assuming closure cancels subscriptions:</strong> Billing systems may require cancellation inside the merchant account.</li><li><strong>Ignoring refunds and chargebacks:</strong> Credits can be missed during reconciliation and leave client balances incorrect.</li></ul><p>Do not issue a dedicated card when the expected spend is too small to justify the administration, the provider cannot support the required controls, or the merchant does not accept the card type. In those cases, a controlled purchase-order process or direct client payment may be cleaner.</p><h2>FAQ: operating a per-client card policy</h2><h3>Should every client receive a separate virtual card?</h3><p>Not always. Separate cards are most useful when clients have different budgets, billing entities, approval rules, or high transaction volume. For a small number of identical low-risk subscriptions, a shared company card with strict merchant controls may be simpler, provided expenses can still be allocated accurately. Never share a card merely to avoid documenting ownership.</p><h3>How often should card limits be reviewed?</h3><p>Review predictable software cards at least quarterly and review active advertising or supplier cards more frequently. Trigger an immediate review when campaign scope changes, a client pauses work, a contractor leaves, a vendor adds usage-based billing, or a card receives an unusual transaction. A review should confirm the purpose, owner, remaining budget, recurring charges, and closure date.</p><h3>Is a reloadable card better than creating a new card for each purchase?</h3><p>It depends on the pattern. Reloadable funding is efficient for repeated purchases within one approved project, while a new temporary card is better for a single unfamiliar merchant or a short-lived access need. Reloadable cards require stronger funding records because the same payment instrument can be used repeatedly. Choose the model that makes the intended boundary easiest to verify.</p><h3>What should happen when a client ends an engagement?</h3><p>Stop new spending, suspend or close the card, remove it from merchant dashboards, and check for pending authorizations, refunds, and recurring subscriptions. Reconcile the final transactions to the client ledger and retain the approval and closure record. If another client or project needs the same vendor, issue or assign access under a new policy rather than silently reusing the old authorization.</p><h3>Can a virtual card replace a written expense policy?</h3><p>No. The card can enforce certain limits, but it does not define ownership, tax treatment, reimbursement responsibility, acceptable purchases, or client authorization. Keep the card policy connected to the expense policy, client agreement, and accounting workflow. Where provider features differ, document the manual control that closes the gap.</p><h2>What to do in the next seven days</h2><p>On day one, list every card and map it to a client, project, owner, merchant, and current purpose. On day two, suspend or review cards with no clear owner or expiry date. On day three, copy the template above into your finance workspace and complete it for one active client.</p><p>During the rest of the week, set limits and reload approvals, document recurring subscriptions, and test your receipt and reconciliation process with recent transactions. Finish by reviewing the policy with the person who approves spending and the person who performs the work. If your workflow needs staged funding, compare a <a href="https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-visa-reloadable">reloadable virtual visa card</a> option against your provider’s controls before moving a critical client account.</p><p>The best policy is not the longest one. It is the one that makes the approved use obvious, limits easy to check, evidence easy to collect, and closure automatic when the client or project changes.</p>
